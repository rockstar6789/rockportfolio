THE CHALLENGE

Large-scale digital transformation is rarely a single customer journey.

It is a connected system of customer and employee experiences, operational workflows, technologies, policies, handoffs, measures and organizational responsibilities.

Challenge / system context visual

The challenge was to create a coherent view of the experience while also understanding how operational teams performed the work behind it, where responsibilities changed hands, and how information moved across the service.

This required moving between multiple levels of detail — from an executive view of the overall service journey to detailed operational workflows that could be inspected, validated and improved.

01

MAKE THE JOURNEY VISIBLE

Create a shared view of how the service actually works.

Complex services become difficult to improve when different teams hold different representations of the same journey.

Service blueprint / journey architecture image

I mapped the service across customer-facing activity, backstage operations, systems, roles and handoffs so that teams could inspect the same underlying service rather than working from isolated artifacts.

The purpose was not documentation for its own sake. The artifacts created a shared reference that could support conversations across customer experience, UX, product, operations, technology and program teams.

02

CONNECT THE JOURNEY TO THE WORK

Connect experience decisions to operational reality.

Customer experience cannot be separated from the workflows, information and operational constraints that produce it.

Operational workflow / service blueprint image

Detailed workflow mapping helped connect experience issues to the work required behind the scenes, including handoffs, responsibilities, systems, dependencies and information requirements.

This allowed design discussions to move beyond isolated touchpoints and consider whether an apparent improvement in one part of the journey might increase effort, risk or complexity somewhere else.

03

RESEARCH THE EXPERIENCE IN CONTEXT

Understand what people do — and the environment in which they do it.

Research becomes more useful when individual experiences can be understood within the larger service system.

Experience research / contextual research image

Research findings were connected to journey stages, operational workflows and service responsibilities rather than treated as isolated observations.

This made it easier to understand not only what people experienced, but why those experiences occurred and where the organization had the ability to intervene.

04

CONNECT VOC AND MEASUREMENT TO THE JOURNEY

Measure the experience where it actually happens.

Measurement becomes more actionable when it is attached to identifiable stages of the service rather than treated as a separate reporting activity.

VoC measurement framework image

I connected research and measurement planning to specific stages of the journey so that teams could understand what should be measured, when, and why.

This creates a stronger relationship between experience signals and the operational conditions that may be producing them.

05

TURN INSIGHT INTO ACTION

Research has value when it changes a decision.

The goal was not simply to collect insight, but to create evidence that could inform product, service and operational decisions.

Insight-to-decision framework image

Journey stages provided a structure for connecting research findings with opportunities, operational issues and decisions.

This helped move the conversation from “what did users say?” toward “what does this mean for the service and what decision should follow?”

06

KEEP THE JOURNEY ALIVE

A journey map should not become a historical artifact.

Services change continuously. Journey management requires a way for artifacts, evidence and decisions to evolve with them.

Journey management lifecycle image

The work therefore considered how journey artifacts could remain useful beyond an individual workshop or project phase.

The objective was to establish a foundation for an evolving service view that could incorporate new research, workflow changes, measures and decisions.

07

DESIGNING FOR GOVERNANCE

Different decisions require different levels of detail.

A single service artifact cannot effectively serve executives, working teams and operational specialists at the same level of abstraction.

L1

Executive

Strategic service view for alignment and decision-making.

L2

Working

Cross-functional service blueprint for collaborative analysis.

L3

Operational

Detailed workflows for inspection, validation and improvement.

L1 / L2 / L3 service blueprint model

Establishing multiple levels of detail allowed the service model to remain coherent while supporting different kinds of organizational decisions.

08

THE NEXT LAYER

AI-enabled Journey Management.

Once journeys, workflows, responsibilities and measures become structured and connected, AI can potentially help teams navigate and maintain that knowledge.

AI-enabled journey management concept

I began exploring how AI agents could help teams understand changes across service blueprints and workflows, surface relevant information and improve access to complex service knowledge.

The opportunity is not to replace service design. It is to make the underlying service knowledge easier to query, maintain and use in everyday decisions.

WHAT I LEARNED

The journey is not the deliverable. Shared understanding is.

The most useful journey-management work creates enough shared understanding for different parts of an organization to make better decisions together.

Maps, workflows, research findings and measures are representations of the service. Their value comes from helping people see relationships that were previously fragmented across teams, systems and organizational boundaries.

The deeper opportunity is to connect these artifacts into a living model of the service that can evolve as the organization learns.
