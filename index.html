THE CHALLENGE

Large-scale digital transformation is rarely a single customer journey.

It is a connected system of customer and employee experiences, operational workflows, technologies, policies, handoffs, measures and organizational responsibilities.

Working within Ontario Health's Patients Before Paperwork transformation, my role involved helping make that complexity visible and actionable across initiatives supporting healthcare service delivery.

The challenge was not simply to create a journey map.

It was to create a shared understanding of how the service works end-to-end and front-to-back — connecting the experience of people using the service with the operational work required to deliver it.

01

Make the Journey Visible

Start by understanding the system

Complex enterprise journeys rarely exist in one application, department or organizational chart.

Understanding the experience required looking beyond individual screens and touchpoints to examine the people, processes and systems involved in delivering the service.

I worked across available documentation, stakeholder and SME knowledge, system information and existing artifacts to progressively develop a clearer representation of the service.

Rather than attempting to capture everything in one enormous diagram, I separated the problem into complementary views designed for different decisions and audiences.

These included:

  • Persona and actor perspectives
  • End-to-end journey views
  • Use cases
  • Operational workflows
  • Service blueprints
  • State changes and handoffs
  • Supporting systems and processes

The objective was not documentation for its own sake.

The objective was to make enough of the system visible that teams could inspect it together, identify gaps and dependencies, and make better decisions.

02

Connect the Journey to the Work

Frontstage experiences depend on backstage operations

Improving a customer or clinician touchpoint in isolation does not necessarily improve the overall service.

A seemingly small frontstage change can create additional work somewhere else.

Removing information from one interaction, for example, may simplify that particular interaction while increasing downstream clarification, administrative effort, delays or rework.

I therefore connected journey-level experience views with more detailed operational workflows.

This created traceability between:

What someone experiences
↓
What they are trying to accomplish
↓
What operational activity must occur
↓
Who performs that work
↓
What information and systems are required
↓
What happens upstream and downstream
FROM

“How can we improve this touchpoint?”

TO

“What happens to the entire service if we change this touchpoint?”

Going from 30,000 feet to operational detail

Different organizational audiences require different levels of visibility.

I developed a three-level service-blueprint approach:

L1

Executive View

A concise representation of the overall service and major stages, designed to support leadership understanding and decision-making.

L2

Working View

A collaborative service-design view connecting actors, experience, activities and major operational dependencies.

L3

Operational View

Detailed workflows showing how work actually moves through roles, processes, systems and state changes.

The views served different purposes while describing the same underlying service.

03

Research the Experience in Context

Evidence before assumptions

Journey artifacts become valuable when they represent evidence rather than organizational assumptions.

My approach combined available research and operational evidence with stakeholder and SME knowledge, then progressively refined the artifacts as additional information became available.

Research and validation activities included methods such as:

  • Stakeholder and SME interviews
  • Workflow and requirements discovery
  • Existing-system and artifact analysis
  • Persona development
  • Use-case development
  • Prototype and concept evaluation
  • Collaborative review and validation
  • Synthesis across multiple information sources

The goal was to establish a shared evidence base while remaining explicit about what was known, assumed, proposed or still required validation.

04

Connect VoC and Measurement to the Journey

A journey should be measurable

Understanding what happens is only one part of Journey Management.

Organizations also need to understand how well the journey is performing and where intervention may be required.

I developed a consolidated VoC research and measurement approach spanning seven stages of the service experience:

Discovery → Conversion Decision → Sign-Up → Setup → Training → Usage → Support

Rather than treating VoC as a separate research activity, the objective was to connect measurement to identifiable stages and moments within the service.

This created a framework for considering different forms of evidence together:

EXPERIENCE EVIDENCE What are people telling us?
BEHAVIORAL EVIDENCE What are people actually doing?
OPERATIONAL EVIDENCE What is happening within the service?
OUTCOME EVIDENCE Is the service producing the intended result?

This makes measurement more useful because a signal can be understood in the context of where it occurs in the journey and what operational activity surrounds it.

05

Turn Insight Into Action

The artifact is not the outcome

A journey map, research report or service blueprint has limited value if teams cannot use it to make decisions.

Throughout the work, I treated artifacts as boundary objects: shared representations that allow people with different responsibilities and levels of technical knowledge to examine the same service.

This meant designing artifacts around the decisions they needed to support.

EXECUTIVE

Where are the major experience and operational risks?

PRODUCT TEAM

What should we prioritize?

SERVICE DESIGN

Where is the experience breaking down?

OPERATIONS

What actually happens when this state changes?

RESEARCH

What do we know, what evidence supports it, and what still needs investigation?

The value of the journey therefore came from its ability to create a common frame through which different teams could align, investigate and act.

06

Keep the Journey Alive

From Journey Mapping to Journey Management

One of the biggest limitations of traditional journey mapping is that the artifact begins aging almost as soon as it is completed.

  • Requirements change.
  • Products evolve.
  • Research produces new evidence.
  • Operational processes change.
  • Measures reveal unexpected behaviour.
  • New stakeholders become involved.

A mature journey-management practice therefore needs more than a finished map.

It requires a mechanism for continually connecting:

Journey
↕
Research & Insights
↕
Operational Workflows
↕
VoC & Measures
↕
Product / Service Decisions
↕
Organizational Change

This changed how I thought about the work.

INSTEAD OF

“Is the journey map finished?”

ASK

“How will the organization keep its understanding of the journey current?”

07

Designing for Governance

Visible. Inspectable. Sequential. Actionable.

As the number and complexity of artifacts increased, consistency itself became an important design problem.

I developed a simple principle for evaluating operational service-design artifacts:

V

Visible

Can people see the important actors, activities and dependencies?

I

Inspectable

Can teams examine the underlying detail when they need it?

S

Sequential

Can people understand how work progresses through the service?

A

Actionable

Does the artifact help someone make a decision or take action?

This helped shift the emphasis from producing visually impressive diagrams toward creating decision-grade artifacts that could support ongoing work.

08

The Next Layer: AI-Enabled Journey Management

What happens when the journey becomes queryable?

As journey-management systems accumulate research, workflows, measures, decisions and organizational knowledge, maintaining the connections manually becomes increasingly difficult.

That creates an opportunity for AI.

Rather than using AI simply to generate another journey map, I am exploring how AI could help organizations maintain and interrogate the journey-management system itself.

For example:

What changed in this workflow since the journey was last updated?

Which journey stages have research evidence but no defined measure?

What downstream activities could be affected by this proposed change?

Which insights have not yet been connected to a product or service decision?

What has changed that frontline teams need to know?

The opportunity is to use AI as an acceleration and knowledge layer over a well-understood service system — not as a replacement for understanding the system in the first place.

WHAT I LEARNED

A journey is not simply something to map. It is something to manage.

Journey Management requires connecting customer and employee experience with the operational reality underneath it.

That means connecting:

People + Journeys + Workflows + Systems + Research + Measures + Ownership + Decisions

— and maintaining those connections as the organization changes.

The goal is not a more detailed journey map. The goal is an organization with a shared, evidence-based and continuously actionable understanding of how its service actually works.
